SUBJECT: Atlantis — Myth, Memory, and the Persistence of a Lost World

        
Entry 001 • Classification: Theoretical / Historical Analysis


        

            

            

              “But afterwards there occurred violent earthquakes and floods; and in a single day and night of misfortune, all your warlike men in a body sank into the earth, and the island of Atlantis in like manner disappeared in the depths of the sea” (Plato, 1998, 25c–d).
            


            

              This is the death of Atlantis as described by Plato in Timaeus: the people of Atlantis succumbing to their corruption and greed, leading to the gods plunging them into the depths of the sea. But while this is Atlantis’s official demise, it continues to live on, shifting from a philosophical teaching into an elusive lost city of gold, hidden at the bottom of the Atlantic Ocean. The fascination with Atlantis persists, but why? Why have we, in pop culture and pseudoscientific spaces alike, found this tale to be so indelible? This essay will examine Atlantis, its evolution into its current state, and the enduring issues that it creates. It will take a close look at the manner in which it took on new life in the 19th century, the racist and nationalist ideologies that propelled it, and how its meaning has continued to evolve.
            


            

              The earliest known mention of Atlantis is credited to Plato, who first mentions it in his writings Timaeus and continues it in his later work Critias. In Timaeus, Plato describes Atlantis as a wealthy empire, shown by walls coated in “tin”, “brass”, and “orichalcum”, along with a temple dedicated to Cleito and Poseidon that was “surrounded by an enclosure of gold” (Plato, 1998, 24e–25a). It is in Timaeus that he also describes its demise: “Atlantis in like manner disappeared in the depths of the sea” (Plato, 1998, 25d). In Critias, Plato describes the greed and corruption of the Atlanteans, stating that “Human nature got the upper hand; they then, being unable to bear their fortune, behaved unseemly” (Plato, 2008, 121b). Critias is also the text in which Plato describes the war between Atlantis and Athens, which is where people have drawn parallels between real historical conflicts and the one described by Plato (Plato, 2008, 120d–121a).
            


            

              While it is unlikely that Plato was giving a true historical account, it is also possible that he was inspired by real conflicts, as it was not unusual for Plato to use metaphors to set moral standards. In the text Republic, he uses the “Noble Lie”, in which people are divided into a hierarchical system based on the metal mixed with their souls (gold, silver, iron, and bronze) as a means of establishing and maintaining order within his ideal city (Morgan, 1998). This demonstrates that, like Atlantis, Plato often used almost fantastical elements to effectively communicate his ideas. It would not be unusual if Plato used the myth of Atlantis to demonstrate how society can fail when it loses virtue in pursuit of imperial power. It demonstrates a clear warning, depicting Atlantis as a wealthy, righteous civilisation, with its continued desire for power and abandonment of virtue ultimately leading to its destruction, while an idealised version of Athens wins the war, retains its morals, and remains intact (Morgan, 1998).
            


            

              For centuries, Atlantis was primarily understood through Plato’s writings, with readers interpreting the story in a multitude of ways, often as a moral tale. However, as we enter the 19th century, attitudes towards the concept of Atlantis begin to change. This evolution coincided with Europeans expanding their presence in other parts of the world. This was driven by the creation of colonies, the East India trading route, and a general fascination with ‘exotic’ lands. Through this new age of exploration, the idea of undiscovered cities and continents became very popular, with authors like Jules Verne featuring Atlantis in his novel Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea in 1869. As the idea of undiscovered lands took hold of the public imagination, so did the idea that Atlantis was real. This development contributed to Atlantis’s rise in popularity, making way for theorists and pseudoarchaeologists to develop a newfound fascination with the elusive Atlanteans. Writer Ignatius Donnelly was among these Atlantis theorists, proposing his own theory in Atlantis: The Antediluvian World (Donnelly, 1882). Donnelly’s theory proposed that Atlantis existed “opposite the mouth of the Mediterranean Sea” (Donnelly, 1882, p. 1), and that “the description of this island given by Plato is not, as has been long supposed, fable, but veritable history” (Donnelly, 1882, p. 1). In his pursuit of scientific evidence, Donnelly turned to the Challenger Expedition in 1872 (Jack, 2024, pp. 32–33). The Challenger Expedition took deep-sea soundings that revealed an underwater mountain range and volcanic peak (Jack, 2024, p. 33). Donnelly pointed to this mountain range as veritable proof of Atlantis’s existence, using the discovery to support his theory that extensive volcanic activity in the area had been powerful enough to sink a continent (Jack, 2024). While the findings of the Challenger Expedition were, in fact, real and vital to scientific understanding, the conclusions drawn by Donnelly were flawed. The problems with Donnelly’s ideas did not only arise from his lack of scientific education; when examining the ideas he puts forward, we begin to see an alarming trend.
            


            

              Today, Donnelly’s theory is easy to dismiss, but at the time, his theory was wildly popular with the press and general public. But why is it a harmful myth to perpetuate? The unfortunate side effect of Donnelly’s theories was his attribution of the creation of numerous civilisations, such as Egypt, to the Atlanteans, stating that Egypt and its pyramids were built as a recreation of Atlantis by colonisers from the sea. Donnelly states that “There is no evidence that the civilisation of Egypt was developed in Egypt itself; it must have been transported there from some other country” (Donnelly, 1882, p. 358). This statement is simply not true, but it plays into an enduring idea that peoples from another world, whether that be Atlantis or outer space, truly built the pyramids. This idea put forward by Donnelly is extremely racist and rooted in the normalised colonial practices of the time. The attribution of Egypt to the Atlanteans shows the displacement these theories caused between non-European cultures and their histories, removing their agency and knowledge by attributing the creation of their civilisations to an imagined external force.
            


            

              This concept of displacement and the underlying assumption are explored in depth in “Rediscovering ‘Lost Cities’ and the Birth of Urban Archaeology: Urban Excavations in the Late Ottoman and Early Mandate Middle East and Their Impact on Developing Archaeological Practices” by Olympia Bobou, Miriam Kühn, and Rubina Raja. The chapter examines the concept that European scholars in the 1800s excavated and ‘rediscovered’ what they called ‘lost cities’ when, in actuality, they were known to local peoples (Bobou et al., 2026, p. 112). By downplaying local knowledge of the cities, European scholars were able to rewrite history for those who were previously unfamiliar with them. ‘Lost’ cities of the Middle East became tourist attractions where it was exceedingly popular to collect artefacts and pieces of the ruins to take home, damaging evidence of the culture and history in that area (Bobou et al., 2026, p. 112). Alongside the disruption of the sites by foreigners, there was also the denial of the locals being involved in the cities. Similarly to Egypt, many ‘lost’ cities are presented as being built and inhabited by an unknown civilisation rather than by the ancestors of the local people.
            


            

              This way of thinking and approaching civilisations is notable when looking at Atlantis. While Atlantis is a fictitious city, the connection that Donnelly draws between it and Egypt is incredibly harmful. This connection, while largely lost in the pop culture evolution of Atlantis, still has broad consequences. Many pseudoarchaeological findings are based on racist, colonial, and nationalist ideas that have been passed down through European archaeological tradition. Theories around places such as Rapa Nui (Easter Island) and Inca civilisations like Machu Picchu attribute their existence or achievements to aliens or external influence. These theories demonstrate how pseudoarchaeological explanations can remove the achievements of ancient peoples from their own histories. While it would be incorrect to say that Donnelly’s approach to Atlantis is fully to blame for this continued ideology, Atlantis and the theories surrounding it contribute to these ideas. While we now associate Atlantis with cartoons and comic books, we must not forget the implications that the myth perpetuates when taken at face value.
            


            

              In conclusion, Atlantis started as a moral tale and evolved into a pseudoarchaeological theory rooted in extremely outdated ideologies. This evolution shows how Atlantis and its purpose have evolved and continue to evolve: first being used to demonstrate the consequences of human greed, then to perpetuate colonial and racist ideas, and finally as a hidden city used as a place of origin for mythical, advanced characters in media. While it is likely that Atlantis will not stop evolving, hopefully the Atlantis of the future will have fully shed its inherited beliefs.
